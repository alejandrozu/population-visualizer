Population, fertility and longevity escape velocity

Ending aging would not make the world fill up

The usual objection to curing aging is that the planet would overflow. The arithmetic says otherwise. Deaths only decide how long people stay; births decide how many people ever arrive. With fertility well below two children per woman, even a world in which nobody dies settles toward a ceiling of roughly one and a half to three times today's population, and then stops. This visualizer is built to let you check that claim, and a second one that follows from it: once longevity escape velocity is reached, a population with a very high median age is not an old population in any sense that matters.

Today's 8 billion grows about 2.5 times and stops, because every generation of births is smaller than the one before.

What sets the ceilingFertility, not longevity

Going from Slow to Fast LEV moves the 2400 population by about 1.3 billion. Changing the fertility preset moves it by tens of billions.

Idea one

Population approaches a ceiling, even if nobody ever dies

Population next year=Population this year+Births-Deaths+Net migration

Longevity escape velocity attacks one term of this identity: deaths. It does nothing directly to births. In the limit where nobody dies, the world's population is simply today's population plus everyone born from now on, and the question becomes whether that total keeps growing or converges.

It converges whenever fertility is below replacement. With no deaths before the end of the childbearing years, replacement in this model is exactly two children per woman: one daughter to replace each mother. At a total fertility rate T, each generation of births is T/2 the size of the generation that produced it. If B is the number of people born in the next generation, the births still to come form a geometric series, B(1 + T/2 + (T/2)2 + ...) = B / (1 - T/2). For any T below 2 that sum is finite. The population climbs, slows and flattens at the people alive today plus that sum.

Longevity decides whether there is a ceiling. Fertility decides where it is.

With today's mortality frozen (the Halt preset), the default fertility path peaks at 9.1 billion in 2049 and falls to about 2 billion by 2200. That is the familiar story of population decline. Medium LEV turns the decline into a plateau, but the height of the plateau barely depends on how fast longevity improves: with Recent trend fertility, Slow, Medium and Fast LEV give 19.2, 19.8 and 20.6 billion in 2400.

The claim has a boundary, and the simulator shows it as readily as the claim itself. The ceiling is low because fertility is low. At replacement-level fertility, abolishing aging does produce open-ended growth: 2.1 forever with Medium LEV passes 30 billion by 2200 and approaches 70 billion by 2400. If fertility settles between about 1 and 1.6 children per woman, as the Recent trend, Floor of 1 and No social rules presets assume, the ceiling sits at 1.5 to 3 times today's population.

Idea two

Under longevity escape velocity, old is not frail

An aging population is normally read as a frail one: more people at ages where disease and death are concentrated. That reading assumes the mortality curve stays where it is while people move along it. Longevity escape velocity is precisely the claim that the curve moves to the right faster than people travel along it, so the calendar stops being a good measure of how old anyone is.

The visualizer measures this with feel age (mortality-equivalent age), which compares risks of dying from disease only. For any life expectancy L, the model builds a disease-only curve: the mortality schedule a population would have if disease were the only cause of death and its life expectancy were L. Someone's feel age is the age on the L = 75 version of that curve with the same annual risk as theirs. With life expectancy 85, a 78-year-old has the disease mortality of a 68-year-old on the 75 curve, so their feel age is about 68. People under 20 keep their actual age.

Default scenario (Medium LEV)

World median age rises from 31 in 2022 to 41 in 2050 and 74 in 2100. Median feel age goes the other way: 33.5, then 27, then 24.

Halt preset

The two measures move together: in 2100 the median age is 50 and the median feel age 53. This is aging in the familiar sense, and it is the only case the "aging crisis" framing describes.

Feel age is computed on disease-only curves, not the generalized ones. Accidents, violence and early-life deaths are not aging, and counting them would make a reckless 25-year-old look biologically older than a careful 60-year-old.

The scale has a floor at 20.

Feel age is only defined from age 20, the youngest adult age on the baseline curve. When longevity is strong enough that most adults have lower disease mortality than a 20-year-old in the life-expectancy-75 world, they cannot be mapped any younger. Instead of piling them all at 20, the model spreads them over ages 20 to 40 with a five-year half-life (20 is twice as likely as 25, 25 twice as likely as 30). A median feel age of 24 to 25 therefore means "as young as this scale can express," not a precise measurement.

Three panels show this: the feel line in Median / Percentile Age, which is on by default, and two panels to switch on under Panels: the Feel-Age Comparison pyramid (actual ages on the left, feel ages on the right) and Health / Feel Age, which maps every actual age to its feel age for any life expectancy you choose.

Births are the engine

The fertility presets, and why each one is there

The total fertility rate (TFR) is a period measure: the number of children a woman would have if she lived through that year's age-specific birth rates. It is not the number of births. Births also depend on how many people are at childbearing ages, which is why a large young generation produces many births even at low fertility. This is population momentum, and it is why every preset keeps the population growing for decades after fertility falls.

Replacement depends on survival. The familiar 2.1 is replacement only when a small share of children die before they have children of their own. With today's world mortality frozen, 2.1 is slightly below replacement and the population eventually shrinks; with no deaths before 50 the bar falls to 2.0.

2.1 forever
2.1 from 2030 onward. The textbook replacement level, included as a benchmark and as a demonstration that "replacement" is a statement about survival as much as about fertility: under Halt it slowly shrinks the world, under any LEV preset it grows it without limit.
UN median
2.1 in 2030, 2.0 in 2050, 1.8 in 2100 and after. A simplified version of the UN's medium projection, which puts world fertility at 2.25 in 2024, just under 2.1 around 2050 and about 1.84 in 2100, with population peaking near 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s. The preset reaches 2.1 earlier than the UN does but ends at the same level. It stands for the official outlook, which the next preset argues is too high.
Recent trend
2.0 in 2030, 1.75 in 2040, 1.6 in 2050, 1.5 in 2100. The default. Official projections have kept assuming that low fertility will level off or recover, and recent data keep coming in below them; the UN's 2024 revision lowered its outlook partly because fertility in large countries such as China turned out lower than expected. This path extends the decline at the pace actually observed. It ends close to the independent IHME forecast published in The Lancet in 2024, which has 1.59 in 2100, and it sits at the low end of that forecast's range for 2050 (1.83, interval 1.59 to 2.08). It is the default because recent data support it best, not because it is the most dramatic.
Floor of 1
2.0 in 2030, 1.75 in 2040, 1.5 in 2050, 1.0 from 2070. Today's lowest-fertility societies become the global norm, but fertility stops falling at about one child per woman, on the view that most people still want a child even when they no longer want several.
No social rules
2.0 in 2030, 1.9 in 2035, 1.6 in 2040, 1.2 in 2050, 0.9 from 2070. What fertility looks like once the social expectations that push people toward parenthood disappear: family pressure, religious norms, the assumption that adults have children. Having children becomes a purely personal preference. Where those expectations have eroded fastest, fertility is already below one child per woman: South Korea's TFR was 0.72 in 2023 and 0.80 in 2025, Taiwan's about 0.7 in 2025, and IHME's forecast keeps both near 0.8 to 0.9 for the rest of the century. The preset settles at 0.9.

Presets are starting points. Selecting one writes the checkpoint years it needs and leaves life expectancy, migration and caution untouched; every number can then be edited. The fertility presets are paths for the world as a whole and are applied unchanged to any place, so a country far from the world average (South Korea at 0.78 in 2022, Niger near 6) jumps toward them; for a single country, edit the TFR column directly.

Deaths decide how long people stay

The longevity presets and longevity escape velocity

Longevity escape velocity (LEV) is the point at which medicine adds life expectancy faster than time uses it up, so that people's remaining life expectancy grows as they age instead of shrinking. Aubrey de Grey gave the idea its standard formulation in 2004, as "actuarial escape velocity": mortality rates falling fast enough that "remaining (not merely total) life expectancy increases with time." In the visualizer it appears as period life expectancy rising by more than one year per calendar year, the usual working proxy for that condition. LEV is treated here as a serious possibility, and the question asked about it is quantitative: if it happens, on what schedule, and with what consequences for population and age?

The Life Expectancy panel colors its line by the annual gain, so the threshold is visible directly: cyan marks gains of 0.8 to 1.2 years per year, around escape velocity, and the two blues anything faster.

Halt
Life expectancy frozen at its 2022 value. The counterfactual of no further progress. Combined with low fertility it produces an early peak and a long decline, and it is the baseline the other presets are measured against.
0.25 forever
A quarter of a year of life expectancy per calendar year, indefinitely. The longest-running trend in demography: female life expectancy in the record-holding country rose "for 160 years at a steady pace of almost 3 months per year" (Oeppen and Vaupel, 2002). This is more of the same, with no acceleration.
Approaching 95
0.25 per year up to 85, then 0.1 per year up to 95, then no further gains. The limits view: progress slows as populations approach a biological ceiling. Olshansky and colleagues argued in 2024 that gains have slowed since 1990 and that radical life extension is implausible this century unless biological aging itself can be slowed, which is precisely the condition the LEV presets assume is met.
Slow LEV
Annual gains of 0.25 to 2030, 0.4 to 2035, 0.8 to 2040, then 1.2. Aging treatments arrive and improve, crossing the escape threshold around 2040, but only just. Life expectancy reaches about 153 by 2100.
Medium LEV
Annual gains of 0.25 to 2030, 0.5 to 2035, 1.0 to 2040, then 1.5. The default. Treatments spread through the 2030s and compound, giving about 97 in 2050 and 172 in 2100.
Fast LEV
The early gains of 0.25, 0.8 and 1.5 per year, then 150 by 2050, then 1.5 per year. A deployment jump: once therapies work, they reach most people within about a decade. The 150 is an absolute level reached by every place, and life expectancy is 225 by 2100.

The presets are defined as annual gains from each place's own 2022 life expectancy, so switching place recomputes them for that place, unless you have edited the life-expectancy checkpoints by hand (adding or removing a checkpoint counts). Every LEV preset keeps its stated rate until the model's ceiling of 500 years, and Approaching 95 gets checkpoints in the years it reaches 85 and 95, so each path keeps the slopes it describes. Preset checkpoints are rounded to the nearest half year; between checkpoints the path is not rounded.

In practice the mortality curve stops improving at a life expectancy of about 456. Beyond that point the background death rate alone, about 0.3 per 1,000 a year at most ages and more around young adulthood, keeps life expectancy below the age-500 limit of the model. Removing that background is the job of the caution lever.

One honest caveat.

Populations have gained more than a year of life expectancy per year before, as Japan did after 1947, but only while deaths in childhood and early adulthood were collapsing. None has yet sustained such gains once early-life deaths were already low, which is what LEV requires. The LEV presets describe what would follow from that technology, not a prediction of when it arrives. Curves for life expectancies of 150 or 500 are extrapolations of the observed shape of human mortality, which is exactly what a scenario about unprecedented longevity requires.

The deaths that aging research does not touch

Caution and migration

Caution: deaths that are not caused by aging

Even a complete cure for aging leaves the deaths that have nothing to do with biological age: accidents, drug overdoses, suicide, homicide and similar causes. Today they are a small share of all deaths. Under LEV they become almost the only deaths left, and they set the long-run floor on mortality. For scale, the WHO counts about 4.4 million deaths a year from injuries of all kinds, including road deaths, suicide and violence: nearly 8% of all deaths, or about 0.55 per 1,000 people per year.

The mortality curves carry that floor too. However high life expectancy goes, a 30-year-old's annual risk of death in the model never falls below about 0.6 per 1,000, because only the aging part of the curve shifts. The caution lever removes it: a caution value c, from 0 to 1, subtracts c deaths per 1,000 from the annual death probability at every age, and rates never go below zero. Once aging deaths are gone, a caution of 1 takes young and middle-aged death rates to zero, which is how the "nobody dies" runs above are produced. The default keeps caution at 0 until 2050 and raises it to 1 by 2100, as aging deaths disappear and the remaining risks become the ones left to eliminate.

Caution is applied after the mortality curve has been fitted to the checkpoint life expectancy. The life expectancy you enter therefore describes mortality before caution; with caution above zero, actual survival is better than that number. In the default scenario the 2100 checkpoint of 172 years corresponds to about 180 once caution 1 is applied. In the Death Curve panel, curves for simulated years include caution; curves added by life expectancy do not.

Migration

Net migration is entered per 1,000 residents per year. In the historical record it is the residual of population change minus births plus deaths, except for the world, where it is exactly zero. In the simulation, the per-mille rate becomes an absolute number each year and is spread across ages with a profile weighted toward young adults and their children: about two thirds of net migrants are 15 to 49 and a quarter are children. World migration can be set to a non-zero value, but only as a hypothetical.

How a scenario is specified

Checkpoints, interpolation and the 2022 anchor

2022 is the present
The source series for 2023 to 2025 are inconsistent across places and providers, so 2022 is the last observed year and everything after it is simulated. Mixing those partial years into the baseline would put artificial jumps into births and deaths.
Interpolation
The 2022 values are always the first point: observed TFR and life expectancy (for groups without their own fertility series, the TFR estimated from their births), and zero migration and caution. Between checkpoints every variable changes linearly, so with a first checkpoint in 2030, 2024 is 75% of the 2022 value plus 25% of the 2030 value. After the last checkpoint the last value is held.
Ordering
Checkpoints sort themselves by year once a year has been typed in full, so a half-typed year never jumps to the wrong position.
The 2022 handoff
The simulation starts from the observed 2022 age structure, births and deaths. The modeled deaths by age are scaled to match the observed 2022 total while keeping the shape of the curve; from 2023 on, deaths come from the curves and the cohorts alone.

What is measured and what is reconstructed

The historical foundation

Long-run pastPopulation series reach back thousands of years. Births, deaths, TFR, life expectancy and age structure are reconstructed where direct series do not exist.
1950-2022The strongest common coverage. Direct international estimates are preferred, and the 2022 age structure seeds the simulation.
2023 onwardEvery value is produced by the visualizer from the chosen assumptions and the inherited cohorts.
Births and TFR: the lower estimate
Where two loaded series disagree on births or on TFR, the lower value is used. This is deliberate: UN estimates of recent births have run above the registered counts in several countries with near-complete registration, by 13.8% for Colombia, Egypt and Türkiye combined in 2022-23 (Fernández-Villaverde and Norrick, 2026), and the UN's own revisions have moved its outlook down. For 1950 to 2022, though, both loaded series come from the UN and agree, so the rule changes nothing in the modern record and the 2022 starting point is the UN estimate. If that estimate is too high, the simulation starts with slightly too many births, and its population results are, if anything, on the high side. The rule applies to the machine-readable series the app loads; it does not scrape other sources.
Births
The lower of the two annual count series where either exists; otherwise a crude birth rate times population, then a stable-population estimate. Counts are bounded to plausible annual rates to suppress isolated source glitches.
Deaths
Direct annual counts first, then regional counts. Where both are missing, deaths are inferred from births minus population change, with a mortality-curve estimate as the last resort.
TFR before the record
Inferred from births, population, growth and the survival implied by that year's life expectancy, then smoothly calibrated to the first observed TFR for the place, so the past joins the record without a jump.
Life expectancy
Direct annual values where they exist, interpolated across gaps, and extrapolated cautiously downward before the first observation.
Age structure
Five-year age groups are interpolated into single years so cohorts are never dumped into one age, the open 100+ group is extended with a declining tail, and the result is rescaled to the total population. Where no age data exist, a stable age distribution is built from growth and mortality.
Historical does not always mean observed.

Before 1950, and for sparse country-years, smooth curves are model-assisted reconstructions. They are good for long-run trajectories, not for claims about a single year.

The annual cohort ledger

How the simulation advances

The engine is a cohort-component model, the standard method of demographic projection, run in single years of age up to 500. Each simulated year follows the same steps.

  1. 1
    Start from one-year cohorts.

    In 2022 these are the observed age structure, smoothed into single years and scaled to the observed population.

  2. 2
    Calculate births.

    Half of each cohort aged 15 to 49 counts as women, and a fixed age pattern of fertility, peaking in the late twenties, turns that year's TFR into births.

  3. 3
    Apply mortality by age.

    Each cohort is multiplied by that year's probability of dying at its age, from the curve for that year's life expectancy with caution applied.

  4. 4
    Age the survivors.

    Survivors move up one year of age and the new births enter at age 0.

  5. 5
    Add net migration.

    The per-mille rate becomes an absolute number and is spread across ages.

  6. 6
    Repeat.

    The new age structure starts the next year, so momentum carries forward automatically.

A life expectancy implies a whole curve

Mortality curves

Life expectancy is a summary of one year's death rates at every age. To turn a life expectancy into deaths, the visualizer builds a complete curve for it. Adult disease mortality follows the Gompertz-Makeham law: a small constant background risk plus a risk that roughly doubles with every eight years of adult age. Infant, childhood, young-adult external and background components are added, scaled to the life expectancy, and the aging (Gompertz) term is shifted along the age axis until the life expectancy the whole curve implies matches the one requested.

This is what lets the model go beyond observed experience. The shift that turns a life-expectancy-75 curve into an 85 curve is the same operation that produces 150 or 500: the rise in risk with age keeps its shape and starts later. A country with life expectancy 55 gets the same treatment in the other direction, with heavier infant and childhood mortality.

Generalized curve

All causes: disease plus infant, childhood, external and background components. It drives deaths, survival to 15 and the population simulation.

Disease-only curve

The disease component alone, fitted so that it by itself gives the chosen life expectancy. It is used only for feel age.

The Death Curve panel uses a hybrid vertical scale: linear from 0 to 0.1 deaths per 1,000, then logarithmic up to 1,000, with the linear band taking the same height as one decade of the log scale. Very low rates stay visible, zero can be shown, and the steep rise in old age is not flattened. The horizontal range ends once every curve shown has reached its maximum (995 deaths per 1,000, less any caution).

A measure built for this tool

Survival-adjusted TFR: the children who reach 15

01

What it measures

TFR counts births. What matters for the next generation is how many of those children live long enough to become parents. The survival-adjusted line multiplies TFR by the probability of surviving from birth to 15, taken from the generalized mortality curve for that place and year. It uses the full 0 to 15 window, not just infant or under-five mortality, because deaths between 5 and 15 were common in the past.

Survival-adjusted TFR = TFR x probability of surviving to age 15

In the high-mortality past the two lines are far apart, which is why high fertility in the past did not mean fast growth. In the model the world's TFR of 4.2 in 1800 is 2.8 once the third of children who died before 15 are removed, and 4.9 in 1950 becomes 3.5. Today the gap is much smaller, and under LEV the two lines become one, which is the cleanest way to see why replacement falls from 2.1 toward 2.0.

It is not a net reproduction rate. It ignores sex-specific survival, deaths between 15 and the age of childbearing, and maternal mortality. Survival to 15 comes from the model's mortality curve, which for recent decades puts child deaths too high: about 9% before 15 for the world in 2022, against about 4.6% observed, so the modern adjusted line runs low.

World, regions and countries

How places are assembled

The place menu runs from World to the continents, then groups (the European Union, ASEAN and the regions of the UN geoscheme, in alphabetical order), then countries. Iran and Afghanistan are grouped with Central Asia, and the European and Oceanian subregions are left out.

A group's population, births, deaths, age structure and migration are summed from its members, and its life expectancy is population-weighted. Where a group has no direct fertility series, its TFR is inferred from its combined births, population, growth and survival rather than averaged country by country. Membership is fixed and does not change over history, so totals can differ from official aggregates.

What the model does not do

Limits

  • It is deterministic. One set of assumptions gives one path; there are no probability ranges.
  • It does not forecast shocks. Wars, pandemics, famines and policy changes appear only if you enter their effects.
  • Mortality follows one family of curves. Many real age patterns share a life expectancy; the model always uses the same smooth shape.
  • The fertility age pattern is fixed. Births do not shift to later ages, even under LEV, where a longer healthy life might change when people have children.
  • Sex is averaged away. Half of each cohort counts as women, pyramids split every age evenly, and mortality is not sex-specific.
  • Migration uses one age profile. It cannot represent a particular refugee, student or retirement flow.
  • Historical precision varies. Early curves come partly from interpolation and demographic identities rather than registration.
  • Sources are live. The data load from Our World in Data each time, so revised source values change results; an exported scenario records results but not the source data.
Best use: compare mechanisms, not headlines.

The most useful conclusions are conditional: with this fertility path, this mortality schedule, this migration and this starting age structure, this follows. Keeping the conditions visible is the point of the tool.

Mortality falling fast enough that remaining life expectancy rises with time; in the app, life expectancy rising by more than one year per calendar year.
Net migration
Immigrants minus emigrants. Negative when more people leave than arrive.
Per mille
Per 1,000 people.
Population ceiling
The level a population approaches when deaths vanish and fertility is below replacement: everyone alive plus a finite sum of future births.
Population momentum
Growth or decline already built into the age structure, which continues for decades after rates change.
Replacement fertility
The TFR at which each generation exactly replaces the one before, absent migration. About 2.1 with modern child survival, 2.0 in this model when nobody dies before 50, and higher where child mortality is high.
TFR
Total fertility rate: the number of children per woman implied by one year's age-specific birth rates.
